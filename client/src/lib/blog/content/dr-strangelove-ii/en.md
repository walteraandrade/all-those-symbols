The fear of AI doom is quite understandable. It is new, and we have been blown away by its capabilities lately. Saying that Fable 5.2 writes better and faster code than 90% of the programmers in the world is no longer bold. Mathematicians, effigies of reason, seeing computers do better than they could ever dream of doing, are starting to get emotional.

## The Enemy
The good part of being in the tech world but having a philosophical background is that I get to see how these worlds are similar from a unique perspective, and I often wander into philosophical ideas while I watch nerds debate the end of the world at the hands of the god of silicon, whom I'd heard of before by a different name: Ogun. Philosophers call one of those ideas "hubris." The word is ancient Greek and means, among a whole lot of other things, "excess" or "imbalance." Its best-known story is one of the coolest of all time: Oedipus, a great hero who, in an irrational and uncontrollable urge to know himself, finds out he has murdered his father and is married to his mother, with whom he even had children. Taken by the horror of knowing this, he gouges out his own eyes, as he does not want to look at his own disgrace anymore.

The heart of the story lies in one question: was Oedipus's tragic end caused by his own choices, or was it his fate, blind to whatever choices and desires Oedipus might have had, set in stone from the very beginning? In the language of the Greeks: did Oedipus bring about his own end, or was it the cruel gods?

This story was written about 500 years before Christ came into town. Fifteen hundred years after he came, a guy named Luther told the same story, claiming that the Catholic Church fell into the "hubris" of power, became corrupt and deviated from Christ's message.

We can say the Garden of Eden is one of these stories.

Most people nowadays are familiar with The Matrix. Some are with Dune, but most of them don't know that the technology in Dune is only that odd because their universe was almost destroyed. From the very first chapter of Dune:

"Once men turned their thinking over to machines in the hope that this would set them free. But that only permitted other men with machines to enslave them."

The absurd genius of Jorge Luis Borges brought his own version of this tale to life through a universe made of an infinite library, one that holds every possible book, 99.99% of them incomprehensible garbage no one can read.

To make art is to dream as a species. I strongly believe that. By creating these delusions, artists can give us a momentary taste of what's coming, to prepare our brains and our imagination to look, even when they are nightmares. These tales often tell us what truly matters: to treasure, care for and cultivate human contact, human intuition and ingenuity.

## The Ally
"Human" is a tough word to define. Some say we have to treat chickens with more humanity, while sometimes defending a more chicken-like life for those who do not offer humanity to chickens. To act in love is human. To act in hate is human. Stalin was no pig, despite Orwell's imagination. Neither was Hitler. And humans were every single one of the people working for them. Brutus was human, all too human, when he betrayed Caesar. So were Judas and Ephialtes.

There are few words that I have a harder time understanding than "Human", when used as a qualification of something or someone. The concern about AI replacing humans is understandable, considering the stories we've been told. But if you are into stories, you know that their purpose is to teach, not exactly to predict.

That is the only reason why, in my opinion, one of the things that save the last season of Game of Thrones is precisely the recognition of the importance of stories, symbolized by the coronation of Bran, who, through crisis, became the one who knows all the stories and, therefore, rules the world.

The stories about AI dominating us look to me the same as these stories. Most people will tell you: "I do not let the AI think for me", and they might also tell you that the media influences a lot of other people, but not them, even when their opinions smell awfully like their bubble's influencers' opinions, often with the same limitations. That means we know what the problem is, and that also means we do not know what the problem is: we can't even define what "to think" is.

For most of history, rationality was considered a trait that humans alone could have. But even though AI marketing campaigns and spotlights may blind us to other inquiries in the sciences, one of the biggest assumptions being questioned right now is exactly to what degree rationality is a human-only trait.[^1] This, I think, is pretty important, as we are in need of some new perspectives on ourselves.

There is a really old book about a poet who lived in Plato's time (around 400 BC) and the book tells a story of a day Socrates wanted to know where poetry came from. Socrates argues that the poet's art comes from the goddesses of inspiration: the Muses, daughters of Zeus. To this day, this story comes up whenever people debate where art comes from and what the artist's role is. In our culture, we learned to praise the artist over his own work, as well as trashing him despite it. This shows us how, as a society, we have moved to regard all achievements of every single individual as a moment of spark and brilliance and to call the people it happens to gods. Sharp is the difference if you know how to read, young padawan: in the first, humans are vessels being carried by the grace of the goddess: the blessing is not in the human; it is in the art itself. As if art is the software running the human in that moment, if you are into these kinds of word plays.

As if to prove a point, Art itself sometimes speaks as a real goddess, like when "Yesterday" came to Paul McCartney in a dream, or "Avôhai" came to Zé Ramalho from a mushroom trip. All these moments, although surrounded by mystery, show us the brilliance of something in us that cannot, by any definition of the word, be called rational.

For this reason, I don't think we'd be doomed even if AI one day does everything reason can do, if that day ever really comes. Most likely, we'll go through a paradigm shift about what we are: as a species, as members of this planet Earth, earthlings, within this so unexplored galaxy. And that will be the twist no story could predict, the paradigm shift that overwrites, in history, even the technological one.

When, in 1608, some revolutionary discoveries in optics got the young nerd Galileo excited to build his new super mega cool telescope, he did it, and had the brilliant idea of pointing it up. And looking.

I believe that, as humans, we'll surely find far better ways to explore the world. We forget that besides being rational, we're also social. Political. Philosophical. And this, I can assure you, is not a vision of optimism.

Personally, I believe that to suffer is a nonnegotiable part of experience. We're hardwired to see problems in the world. Philosophically, I believe that whatever happens at the "AI singularity," we will still find problems to solve. Science, we forget, is one way to know and to interact with the world. Our times have made a god of it, as if it delivered certainty. It doesn't, and it can't. A theory predicts certain observations; when they occur, we take them as evidence that the theory is true. But evidence is not proof: concluding a theory from its confirmed predictions is, logically, affirming the consequent. Another theory could predict the very same observations.

A simple example makes it clearer: for centuries, Europeans observed only white swans. They concluded that all swans were white. After all, if all swans are white, the swans we observe should be white. And they were. Until Europeans reached Australia and found black swans.

![Interactive widget: observing white swans one by one never proves that all swans are white, the argument stays invalid; one black swan from Australia refutes it, and that argument is valid.](#black-swan)

The observations were real. The conclusion was not necessarily true. No number of white swans can prove that all swans are white, but a single black swan proves that they are not. Hume saw the problem in the eighteenth century, and Popper built his philosophy of science around this asymmetry: science does not prove theories, it keeps the ones that have not been refuted yet.

This fundamental limit does not make Science less awesome. It only means that it is a particular way of inquiring and describing the world. When we use language, we are doing this in another way, through sounds and/or words. When we play songs, we are doing the same. When we hug or kiss, that's yet another way of inquiring and describing the world.

That realization is something we might also have dreamed of in the fifties and sixties, when people took drugs so intensely that they were often in a non-rational state, and their answer to a world in the grip of the bomb crisis was love.
